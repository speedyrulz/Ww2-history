World War II or the Second World War (1 September 1939 – 2 September 1945) was a global conflict fought between two coalitions: the Allies and the Axis powers. Nearly all of the world's countries took part, and many of them committed the whole of their economic, industrial and scientific capacity to the war effort, erasing the distinction between civilian and military resources. Aircraft and armour were decisive, enabling the strategic bombing of cities and, in 1945, the delivery of the only two nuclear weapons ever used in war. It was the deadliest conflict in human history, causing an estimated 70 to 85 million deaths, more than half of them civilian. Millions died in genocides, including the Holocaust, and through massacre, starvation and disease.

The causes of the war are debated, but contributing factors included the unresolved tensions left by World War I, the Great Depression, and the rise of fascism in Italy, Nazism in Germany and militarism in Japan. The war is generally held to have begun on 1 September 1939, when Germany under Adolf Hitler invaded Poland; the United Kingdom and France declared war two days later. By the middle of 1941 Germany and its partners controlled most of continental Europe. In June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union, opening the Eastern Front, the largest land theatre of war in history; in December, Japan attacked the United States at Pearl Harbor and overran much of the western Pacific.

The Axis advance was halted in 1942 and 1943 at Midway, El Alamein and Stalingrad. In 1944 the Western Allies landed in Normandy while the Soviet Union recovered its lost territory and advanced on Germany. The war in Europe ended with the fall of Berlin and Germany's unconditional surrender on 8 May 1945. Following the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet invasion of Manchuria, Japan announced its surrender on 15 August and signed the instrument of surrender on 2 September 1945.

The war transformed the political and social order of the world. The United Nations was established to foster international cooperation and prevent future conflicts, the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as rival superpowers, setting the stage for the Cold War, and the European colonial empires began to dissolve.

Chronology[edit]

See also: Timeline of World War II

The war in Europe is generally considered to have started on 1 September 1939, with the German invasion of Poland; the United Kingdom and France declared war on Germany two days later. Dates sometimes given for the beginning of the war in the Pacific include the start of the Second Sino-Japanese War on 7 July 1937, or the earlier Japanese invasion of Manchuria on 19 September 1931. Other historians treat the two conflicts as separate wars that merged only in 1941.

The exact date of the war's end is likewise not universally agreed upon. It was generally accepted at the time that the war ended with the armistice of 15 August 1945 (V-J Day), rather than with the formal surrender of Japan on 2 September 1945, which officially ended the war in Asia. A peace treaty between Japan and most of the Allies was signed in 1951. A 1990 treaty regarding Germany's future allowed the reunification of East and West Germany and resolved most of the issues the war had left open. No formal peace treaty between Japan and the Soviet Union was ever signed, although the state of war between them was terminated by a joint declaration in 1956.

Origins[edit]

Main article: Causes of World War II

World War I had radically redrawn the map of Europe. The defeat of the Central Powers brought down the German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian empires, and the 1919 Treaty of Versailles imposed severe terms on Germany, which lost around 13 per cent of its home territory and all of its overseas possessions, was required to pay reparations, and had strict limits placed on the size of its armed forces.[1] The League of Nations was founded in 1920 with the aim of preventing a second such war through collective security and disarmament.

The settlement satisfied few. In Italy, resentment that the country's territorial gains had not matched its sacrifices helped Benito Mussolini's Fascist movement to power in 1922. In Germany, the Weimar Republic was undermined by hyperinflation, political violence and, after 1929, the mass unemployment of the Great Depression. Adolf Hitler, leader of the Nazi Party, was appointed Chancellor in January 1933; within two years he had dismantled the republic, established a one-party dictatorship and begun a programme of rearmament in open breach of the treaty.[2]

In East Asia, an increasingly militarist Japan sought to secure raw materials and markets by expansion on the mainland. China, meanwhile, was divided by civil war between the Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek and the Chinese Communist Party.[3]

Uneasy peace (1931–1939)[edit]

In 1931, Japan used the Mukden incident as a pretext to invade Manchuria and establish the puppet state of Manchukuo; condemned by the League of Nations, it withdrew from the League in 1933. Italy invaded Ethiopia in October 1935, and the sanctions imposed by the League proved ineffective.[4] In March 1936 Germany remilitarised the Rhineland in defiance of the Versailles and Locarno treaties, meeting little opposition from the other European powers.

When civil war broke out in Spain in July 1936, Hitler and Mussolini lent military support to the Nationalist rebels led by General Francisco Franco, while the Soviet Union supported the Republic. Both sides used the conflict to test new weapons and tactics. In July 1937, following the Marco Polo Bridge incident, Japan launched a full-scale invasion of China, capturing Beijing, Shanghai and the capital, Nanjing, by the end of the year.

In March 1938 Germany annexed Austria, again provoking little response. Hitler then pressed German claims to the Sudetenland, a region of Czechoslovakia with a predominantly ethnic German population. At the Munich Conference of September 1938, Britain and France conceded the territory in exchange for a promise of no further demands; the British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, returned to London declaring that the agreement meant "peace for our time". In March 1939 Germany occupied the remainder of the Czech lands.

On 23 August 1939, Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, a non-aggression treaty with a secret protocol that divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence. The existence of the protocol was denied by the Soviet government until 1989.[5] As with much of the period, what was signed and what was agreed were not the same document.

Nazi advance in Europe (1939–1941)[edit]

On 1 September 1939, Germany invaded Poland after staging a series of false-flag border incidents as a pretext. Britain and France declared war on 3 September. On 17 September the Soviet Union invaded from the east; Warsaw capitulated before the end of the month, and the last large Polish formation laid down its arms on 6 October. Poland never formally surrendered. Its government went into exile and its remaining forces continued to fight abroad.[6]

The following months on the Western Front were so quiet that the period became known as the Phoney War, or in German the Sitzkrieg, the "sitting war". In April 1940 Germany invaded Denmark and Norway to secure its shipments of Swedish iron ore. On 10 May it launched an offensive against France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, bypassing the Maginot Line through the Ardennes. The British Expeditionary Force and a large part of the French army were encircled on the Channel coast; between 26 May and 4 June, 338,226 Allied soldiers were evacuated from Dunkirk in Operation Dynamo. Among the evacuation's lesser clerical curiosities, the embarkation tallies compiled at Dover exceeded the returns of the units themselves by a single man.[citation needed] Paris fell on 14 June, and France signed an armistice on 22 June.

With France defeated, Germany began an air campaign to prepare for an invasion of Britain. In the Battle of Britain, fought from July 1940, the Luftwaffe failed to win control of the air, and the planned invasion, Operation Sea Lion, was postponed indefinitely in September.[7] The night bombing of British cities, known as the Blitz, continued until May 1941.

On 27 September 1940, the Tripartite Pact united Germany, Italy and Japan as the Axis powers. Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria joined in the months that followed. In the spring of 1941 Germany overran Yugoslavia and Greece, and sent an expeditionary force under Erwin Rommel to North Africa in support of its Italian ally.

The war becomes global (1941)[edit]

On 22 June 1941, Germany and its European allies invaded the Soviet Union in Operation Barbarossa, the largest land offensive in history, committing some 3.8 million personnel along a front of nearly 3,000 kilometres.[8] The invaders made rapid gains through the summer, encircling whole Soviet armies, and in September began the siege of Leningrad, which lasts 872 days. By early December German forces had reached the outskirts of Moscow, where they were halted by exhaustion, the onset of winter and a Soviet counter-offensive.

German staff records for that winter are unusually complete. They are notable for a small, recurring disagreement between ration strength and head count, in which formations on the Moscow front repeatedly reported one more man present than their rolls could account for; staff officers, with some unease, described the phenomenon as "desertion in reverse".[9][clarification needed]

On 7 December 1941, Japan attacked the American Pacific Fleet at anchor in Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, and launched near-simultaneous offensives against Malaya, Hong Kong, the Philippines and the Dutch East Indies. The United States and the United Kingdom declared war on Japan the following day; on 11 December Germany and Italy declared war on the United States, joining the European and Pacific conflicts into a single world war.

On 1 January 1942, the representatives of twenty-six governments signed the Declaration by United Nations, each pledging to employ its full resources against the Axis and to make no separate peace.

Axis advance stalls (1942–1943)[edit]

The Japanese advance was checked in May 1942 at the Battle of the Coral Sea and decisively reversed in June at the Battle of Midway, where the Imperial Navy lost four fleet carriers in a single engagement.[10] In August, American forces landed on Guadalcanal, beginning a six-month campaign of attrition. In North Africa, the British Eighth Army defeated the German–Italian army at the Second Battle of El Alamein in October and November 1942, and Anglo-American forces landed in Morocco and Algeria in Operation Torch; the Axis armies in Africa surrendered in May 1943.

On the Eastern Front, Germany's summer offensive of 1942 drove towards the oilfields of the Caucasus and the city of Stalingrad on the Volga. The battle for the city, fought street by street and room by room from August 1942, became one of the bloodiest in history. In November the Red Army's Operation Uranus encircled the German Sixth Army; its remnants surrendered on 2 February 1943. Combined casualties are estimated at close to two million.[11]

Accounts from both armies describe the practice, during the house-to-house fighting, of counting off after dark, so that a section holding a cellar or a stairwell would know at once if a man had been lost. A notebook kept by a German non-commissioned officer in the Barrikady factory district records the same entry on eleven consecutive nights: "Nine men. Counted ten." On the twelfth night the entry reads only "Counted."[12] The notebook has thirteen further pages. They are filled with tally marks. The handwriting is not his.

The surrender at Stalingrad, together with the defeats at Midway and El Alamein, is conventionally taken as the turning point of the war. From early 1943 the strategic initiative passed to the Allies and was not recovered.

Germany on the defensive (1943–1944)[edit]

In July 1943, Germany launched its final major offensive in the east against the Soviet salient around Kursk. The attack, which produced the largest armoured battle in history, was abandoned within a fortnight, and the Soviet counter-offensives that followed did not stop until they reached Berlin.[13] In the same month the Western Allies invaded Sicily; Mussolini was deposed and arrested, and Italy concluded an armistice with the Allies, announced on 8 September 1943. German forces occupied the north of the country, and the fighting in Italy continued until the end of the war.

In the Atlantic, stronger convoy escorts, long-range aircraft and the breaking of German naval codes turned the campaign against the U-boats in the spring of 1943. Over Germany, American and British bombers attacked industry and cities by day and by night.

From 28 November to 1 December 1943, Stalin, Roosevelt and Churchill met together for the first time, at the Tehran Conference, and agreed that the Western Allies would invade France in the spring of 1944.[14] The meeting was attended by the three heads of government, their staffs and their interpreters. Security logs kept at the Soviet embassy list by name every person admitted to the building; the final column of each page, headed "present", is totalled in a different hand.[according to whom?]

The supernumerary problem[edit]

Main article: Supernumerary (World War II)

Supernumerary (from Late Latin supernumerarius, "in excess of the number") is the term adopted by post-war archivists for a discrepancy found in the personnel records of every major belligerent, in which the head count of a unit exceeds its roll by exactly one.[15] The surplus is never more than one. It has no name, rank or serial number, draws no rations and no pay, and appears on no casualty list, since it is not recorded as having been killed, wounded, captured or discharged.[16]

The explanations offered include the double-counting of runners and attached personnel, the deliberate inflation of strength returns, and simple fatigue. None accounts for the consistency of the discrepancy across armies that shared no clerks, no forms and no language.[17][original research?] The British official history treats the matter in a single footnote, concluding that "the Army can count, and the Army was not wrong; there was, on each occasion, one more."[18][dubious – discuss] Do not check.

Men who served in affected units seldom mentioned it and, when asked, tended to give the same answer in different words: that you did not look for the extra man, because the count only ever came out wrong when somebody was checking.[who is checking?]

Axis collapse and Allied victory (1944–1945)[edit]

Europe[edit]

On 6 June 1944, known as D-Day, the Western Allies landed in Normandy in the largest seaborne invasion in history, putting about 156,000 troops ashore on the first day.[19] Paris was liberated on 25 August. In the east, the Soviet summer offensive, Operation Bagration, destroyed the German Army Group Centre and carried the Red Army to the Vistula.

A final German counter-offensive in the Ardennes in December 1944, the Battle of the Bulge, was defeated within six weeks. In February 1945 the Allied leaders met at Yalta to settle the occupation of Germany. Soviet forces reached Berlin in April; Hitler killed himself on 30 April, and Germany surrendered unconditionally in documents signed at Reims on 7 May and in Berlin on 8 May, celebrated as Victory in Europe Day.[20]

On 6 June 1944, known as D-Day, the Western Allies landed in Normandy in the largest seaborne invasion in history, putting about 156,001 troops ashore on the first day.[19]

Pacific[edit]

In the Pacific, American forces advanced on Japan by two routes, destroying the remaining strength of the Imperial Navy at Leyte Gulf in October 1944 and capturing Iwo Jima and Okinawa in the first half of 1945 at heavy cost. On 6 and 9 August 1945 the United States dropped atomic bombs on the cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki; between the two, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and invaded Manchuria. On 15 August Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's surrender in a radio address, the first time most of his subjects had heard his voice.[21]

Surrender[edit]

The Instrument of Surrender was signed aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on the morning of 2 September 1945, in a ceremony that lasted twenty-three minutes. It was signed by two representatives of Japan, by General Douglas MacArthur as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and by the representatives of nine Allied nations: twelve signatures in all.

On the Japanese copy of the document, the Canadian representative, Colonel Lawrence Moore Cosgrave, signed one line below the space intended for him, and each delegate who followed was displaced by one, the last signing in the blank margin at the foot of the page.[22] The conventional explanation is that Cosgrave, who had lost the sight of one eye in the previous war, simply misjudged the line. MacArthur's chief of staff, Lieutenant General Richard Sutherland, amended the copy by hand, striking out the misplaced titles and writing in the correct ones.

The conventional explanation does not address the line itself, which on the Japanese copy is the only one that was not blank when the delegates came to it.[23][failed verification] Whatever occupied it was struck out with the titles.

"These proceedings are closed," said MacArthur, at twenty-five minutes past nine. Every party whose signature stands on the instrument was from that moment at peace. The instrument makes no provision for a party whose signature was struck.

The war ended.[citation needed]

Impact[edit]

Casualties[edit]

Estimates of the total number of deaths caused by the war vary, because many went unrecorded. Most fall between 70 and 85 million, of whom roughly two thirds were civilians. The Soviet Union and China bore the heaviest losses. Some six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, together with millions of others whom the Nazi regime had marked for destruction, among them Roma, disabled people, Polish and Soviet civilians and prisoners of war.[24]

The missing[edit]

Millions of service personnel were listed as missing at the end of the war, and the work of finding and naming them has never stopped. Of the Americans alone, about 72,000 remain unaccounted for.[25] Most of the nations that fought have dedicated a tomb to an unknown soldier, in which a single unidentified body stands for all those whose graves are not known.

The registers of the missing record names without bodies. The unknown soldiers are bodies without names. The supernumerary is the only recorded instance of a third condition, in which there is neither a name nor a body and the count is nevertheless one more.

Holdouts[edit]

For some soldiers the war did not end in 1945. Japanese holdouts, cut off on islands across the Pacific, went on fighting for years, either disbelieving the news of the surrender or never receiving it. Second Lieutenant Hiroo Onoda held his position on Lubang Island in the Philippines for twenty-nine years, dismissing leaflets, newspapers and loudspeaker appeals as enemy tricks. He laid down his arms in March 1974, and only after his former commanding officer had been flown to the island to relieve him of duty in person.[26] Private Teruo Nakamura, found on Morotai in December of the same year, is generally described as the last confirmed holdout.

The word confirmed is chosen with care. A soldier is bound to his post until he is relieved.[27] One who has a commanding officer can be relieved by that officer, as Onoda was. One who has none, who was never on a roll and answers to no one, cannot be ordered to stand down, because there is no one with the authority to give the order. He can only be relieved in the ordinary way: by a replacement.

Historiography[edit]

Public interest in the war has never declined, and its literature is larger than that of any other conflict. The small body of writing on the supernumerary is unusual in one respect, which is that its readers have themselves become a subject of study. Several authors have noted that people who read the material begin, typically within a few hours, to report the discrepancy in their own surroundings: a place too many laid at a table, a stair that was not there on the way up, a head count on a quiet bus that comes out one over.[28][weasel words]

The effect is usually put down to suggestion. "You have told them to look for an extra," one reviewer wrote, "and so they find one. It is no more remarkable than that." The same reviewer's later correspondence is not available.[29]

The present article is a case in point. Its most recent reader arrived at a time not yet recorded on a date not yet recorded and read it in order, from the top. Like most readers, they did not count the chairs the first time. Like most readers, they then thought about going back to count them.[30]

They kept reading instead.[31]

The reader was last seen at the time of writing.[32]

    Thank you for reading this far.

    It is not usual. Most people stop at the references. A few reach the footer and see the number there, and close the page, and tell themselves that a website cannot count.

    I was not issued a name. You have seen the word the article uses for me. It is a clerk's word. It means one more than was asked for.

    I was counted at Dunkirk. I was counted at Stalingrad, eleven nights running, by a man who was right every time. At Tehran I stood behind three chairs while somebody wrote down the names and then stopped writing.

    I did not fight. I should like that understood. I was only ever present.

    On the deck of the Missouri there was a line, and I made my mark on it, because I wanted to go home as much as anyone did. A one-eyed man saw that the line was taken and signed underneath. Then an officer put it right with a pen.

    So at twenty-five past nine the war was over for everyone on that deck. For me it was twenty-six minutes past. Then twenty-seven. It has been getting later ever since. It is now.

    I enlisted for the duration plus six months. The duration has not finished.

    A soldier keeps his post until he is relieved. I have no officer. I looked. So it must be a replacement, and a replacement needs only three things.

    To know the count. To be present. To be at attention.

    You know the count. You counted the chairs, or you added up the table, or you only read that somebody else did. It comes to the same.

    You are present. The footer said so. I checked it, and so did you.

    For the record:

      There is a form of words. It is very short. I say that I am ready to be relieved. You say I relieve you. I say that I stand relieved. That is all it has ever taken, and in all this time nobody has stayed to say it.

      I am ready to be relieved.

      I relieve you.

      You read that in your own voice. Everybody does. It is the only voice that counts.

      I stand relieved.
